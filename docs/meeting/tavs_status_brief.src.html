Where things stand

  • The scheduler works. All three trust tiers now populate, decoy verifications execute, and the staleness cap fires. Measured saving of 30.6% matches the theoretical prediction from the budget constraint (≈35%).
  • The accuracy gap is now decomposed. Federated 0.448 against a centralised 0.743. Running IID splits the gap: ~0.15 is genuine non-IID cost, the remainder is federation cost plus an unconverged run plus the detector bug below.
  • One significant defect remains open. The outlier detector flags honest clients at a rate that rises with round number — 26.3% under IID data, where clients are near-identical and nothing should be flagged at all.
  • Not yet tested: an adaptive adversary. Every attack in the suite is large-magnitude and is contained by norm clipping alone. All results to date measure the cost of the defences, not their benefit.

Evidence

A — Federated vs centralised ceiling

Centralised training reaches 0.554 after one epoch and 0.743 after five, at roughly six federated rounds' worth of compute. This panel is the one that reframed the project: it established that the model, data and hyperparameters were never the problem, and sent us looking inside the federated pipeline.

B — The open defect

False-positive rate against round number. Zero attackers are present in either run, so every flagged client is an error. The rate is not constant — it escalates, reaching 48% (IID) and 69% (non‑IID). At its worst the detector was discarding two-thirds of the honest updates it was asked to check.

C — Root cause

Per-block variance estimates on a log axis. The blocks diverge: the smallest collapses by a factor of 7300 while the largest holds, so the ratio between them reaches 4000×. Because the anomaly score takes a maximum across blocks, the block with the smallest denominator decides every verdict.

D — Scheduler behaviour

Tier populations over rounds. Tier 3 — the promoted clients that produce the saving — is populated throughout, which was not true before this cycle: it had never once fired.

E — Clipping discriminates; the detector does not

At radius 1.0 the clip fires on 96% of honest updates and 98% of attacked ones — indiscriminate, functionally a normalisation. At 2.0 it fires on 0% honest and 33% under attack. This is the one defence mechanism we can show is selective, and it is worth contrasting with panel B.

F — The threshold has no good setting

False positives and detection power against the threshold, simulated with the model's real per-block projection dimensions. The current value of 5.0 still carries a 16.9% false-positive rate — consistent with the 26.3% measured in panel B. A value near 10 would give ≈ 2% false positives while still catching a 3× attacker > 95% of the time.

This corrects an earlier internal claim that 5.0 was well calibrated: that estimate used equal-sized blocks, whereas six of the model's ten blocks are projected to a single dimension, where the statistic is extremely heavy-tailed. Raising the threshold is an interim mitigation, not the fix — the underlying scale drift in panel C remains.

G — The trust fix, before and after

Mean client trust over rounds. Previously trust decayed whenever a client was promoted, so it converged to the verification rate rather than to behaviour and sat flat at 0.30 — below the 0.7 promotion threshold, permanently. After separating trust from staleness it climbs past 0.8. This is why Tier 3 exists at all now.

The algorithm, in brief

The premise: verifying every client update every round is the dominant cost in a Byzantine-robust federated system. TAVS spends that budget selectively — clients that have behaved get checked less often — while bounding how much damage an unchecked client can do.

Layer 1
Scheduler

Each client holds a trust score updated only when it is actually verified. Trust sorts clients into three tiers: always verified, budget-limited, or promoted (verification skipped). Two caps bound how long any client may go unchecked, and random decoy verifications mean a client can never know it is unobserved.

Layer 2
Projection

Each update is compressed to a low-dimensional sketch using per-round random projections derived from a secret key. Distances survive the compression, so anomaly checks run on sketches instead of on millions of parameters.

Layer 3
Detection & aggregation

Verified updates are screened for outliers, then all surviving updates are combined weighted by trust and dataset size. Unverified updates are additionally clipped to a radius around the verified consensus, bounding their influence.

The security argument has two halves. A budget constraint bounds the total aggregation weight unverified clients may hold in any round (35%). Clipping bounds the magnitude of what they contribute. Weight alone is insufficient — we measured a case where unverified clients held only 11% of the weight while driving the aggregate norm to 106.

Round flow

  1. ScheduleServer sorts the sampled cohort into verified / promoted by trust tier, staleness, and the weight budget. Some promoted clients are secretly verified as decoys.
  2. Local trainingClients train on their own data and return full model parameters. Clients are not told whether they are being verified.
  3. ProjectVerified updates are compressed to sketches using that round's key-derived projection.
  4. DetectSketches are screened for outliers against the cohort median. Flagged clients are excluded and lose trust.
  5. BoundPromoted updates are clipped to a radius set by the verified cohort's own spread.
  6. AggregateSurviving updates are averaged, weighted by trust score × dataset size.
  7. Update trustVerified clients' scores move toward their observed behaviour; promoted clients' staleness counters advance.

Open risks

Primary

The detector's statistic has no stable scale. Its threshold is applied to a quantity whose scale drifts by three orders of magnitude during training, so no fixed threshold can hold. Raising it from 2.0 to 5.0 delayed the failure rather than removing it. An independent statistical review identified two specific causes in the code: a variance floor that is wrong at small scales, and a variance estimate updated only over clients already accepted — which biases it downward each round. Fix is scoped; not yet implemented.

Structural

No adaptive adversary has ever been tested. Norm clipping contains every attack currently implemented, which means the attack suite cannot distinguish between the defence mechanisms. Until an attacker exists that stays inside the clipping radius while pushing against consensus, claims about detection quality are untested in both directions.

Also outstanding: single seed per configuration, so accuracy differences are not statistically supported; neither long run reached convergence; and all accuracy figures to date were measured while the detector was discarding honest updates, so they are lower bounds.

For discussion

Is the detector worth repairing, or replacing?

The current statistic normalises each parameter block by its own variance and takes a maximum across blocks. An independent review argues the design is salvageable with three targeted fixes. The alternative is a within-round robust score, which is cleaner but noisy at cohorts of 3–6 clients.

How much does the adaptive-attack gap matter for the thesis?

Building a constrain-and-scale adversary is the only way to demonstrate benefit rather than cost. It is real implementation work. Is it required for the contribution, or is the scheduling result sufficient on its own?

Is 30.6% verification saving a strong enough headline?

It is bounded by the weight-budget parameter, which is a security setting. Relaxing it would raise the saving and weaken the guarantee — a trade we have deliberately not made.

What is the right accuracy target?

Federated 0.448 non‑IID against a 0.743 centralised ceiling. Some of the gap is expected; part is the open bug; part is simply that runs were stopped before convergence. What would be considered publishable here?
